Two Rovers, One Map

Two rovers start in different rooms knowing nothing about each other. They find each other by both looking at the same wall markers, then merge their maps. Every picture below came out of a run — nothing is illustrative.

Section 02 · the films

Watch it happen

Three recordings of the same office run - the one after the fixes - each answering a different question. Maps were captured every 4 s of simulated time and the rovers are drawn from their 2 s position logs, so you are watching them drive rather than teleport.

A merge can look visibly off — about 15° — and the reason is marker placement, not the algorithm.

The four markers both rovers saw span 13.8 m north–south but only 3.0 m east–west. Nearly collinear landmarks fix position along the line and leave rotation poorly conditioned, so one mediocre landmark at the end of it levers the whole angle. Each landmark was individually good — five of six within 0.21 m of truth — and a plain fit over the same four gives 0.37 m / 0.6°. What was published was 2.25 m / 14.8°.

Two things follow. The shared markers must enclose area, not describe a line — that rule is now on the lab card. And the aligner is discarding accuracy its own landmarks contain, which happened on the office run too; that is a fix waiting to be made, and it needs no simulator to test.

Section 04 · the honest part

Will any of this work on real rovers?

Not on the first attempt — and the reason is not the part this session spent its night on. Here is what the simulation earned the right to claim, split by how much I would bet on each.

Should transfer
  • The marker to landmark to transform chain. It runs the same OpenCV detector node the rover already uses, on real rendered images. Measured 1.5 cm at 1.5 m.
  • The numbers on the lab card. 200 mm square, 0.20 marker length, 4.5 m range cap, 300 mm height — derived by measurement, not assumption.
  • The topic contract and the vetoes. Requiring tag evidence, and refusing a transform when tags and grid disagree, is platform-independent logic — and it caught a confident 180° error live.
Will be worse in reality
  • Alignment accuracy, because it is bounded by map drift and the simulated lidar is better than the real one. Gazebo returns a clean constant scan; the RPLidar changes its ray count every revolution — this repo contains scan_normalizer.py written specifically because that broke SLAM on hardware. The sim never exercised it.
  • Marker detection under motion. No motion blur, no rolling shutter, no exposure changes, no calibration error. A turning rover will do worse.
  • Marker visibility. The simulated plates are readable from both faces — a deliberate simplification to remove a yaw ambiguity. A printed marker is one-sided, so rovers will share fewer markers than here.
Never tested at all
  • The network. Everything ran on one machine over loopback. There is a recorded finding on this branch that our own DDS traffic starves the rover's firmware. This is the part I would expect to fail first.
  • Clock sync between rovers. Peer poses are TF lookups across two machines; 50 ms of skew is centimetres at walking pace and much worse mid-turn. The simulation shared one clock.
  • Compute budget. A desktop with a discrete GPU ran what a Jetson will have to run — SLAM, Nav2, cloud filter and the detector at once.
  • Lighting, floors, people. Uniform light, flat ground, no thresholds, nobody walking through.
What I would do before booking a lab day — in this order. None of it needs two rovers.
  1. Print the markers and replay a real rover bag through the detector. One afternoon, and it validates marker size, lighting and motion blur on real optics at once.
  2. Check clock sync between the two rovers, and put two machines on the lab LAN publishing map-sized messages to test the bandwidth plan.
  3. Feed the simulator replayed real scans — the repo already has the replay harness and the scan normalizer — to get a drift number that means something.

The order is deliberate: the cheapest checks target the risks the simulation could not see, and the expensive thing this session already validated — the alignment maths — is what I am least worried about.
